The bicycle is the most efficient machine ever built for moving a human
being. A cyclist converts roughly ninety percent of the energy in each
pedal stroke into forward motion, a figure no engine has matched. The
secret is the chain drive: two sprockets of different size, linked by
steel, trading force for speed with almost no loss to heat.

Early bicycles were absurd. The penny-farthing put the rider atop a wheel
nearly five feet tall, because gears did not exist yet and speed came
only from wheel diameter. Stopping meant climbing down, and a pothole
meant a header over the handlebars. The safety bicycle of the 1880s,
with two equal wheels and a chain, ended all that in a decade.

Modern frames are drawn from aluminum or carbon fiber, but the geometry
has barely changed in a century: the diamond frame is a truss, and a
truss is the stiffest way to connect four points. Tire pressure matters
more than most riders think. A soft tire deforms against the road and
bleeds energy; a hard tire skips over bumps and loses grip. The fast
setup is the one that matches the surface.

Gears are about cadence, not strength. The strongest riders spin near
ninety revolutions per minute and let the derailleur do the leverage.
Climbing is arithmetic: watts divided by kilograms, minus the wind. On a
steep grade the air barely matters; on the flat it is everything, which
is why racers ride in a line, each sheltering in the slipstream of the
rider ahead.

Maintenance is simple and honest. A clean chain, true wheels, and brake
pads with life left in them will carry a bicycle across a continent.
The machine asks for little and gives back exactly what you put in.
