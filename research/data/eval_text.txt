The workshop smelled of cut pine and machine oil. Mara set the brass
compass on the bench and turned it over in her hands, watching the needle
settle. Outside, rain drummed against the corrugated roof, and somewhere
in the rafters a sparrow had built a nest out of twine and receipts.

She had inherited the shop from her grandfather, along with his notebooks:
page after page of careful diagrams, measurements recorded to the
millimeter, and marginal notes in a hand that grew shakier year by year.
One entry, dated fourteen years before she was born, described a clock
that would run for a century on a single winding. He had never finished
it. The half-built movement still sat in the corner cabinet, its gears
wrapped in oiled cloth.

Mara unwrapped it that evening. The brass was dull but sound, the teeth
of each wheel cut with a patience she could feel in her fingertips. She
cleaned every part twice, then a third time, until the metal shone like
honey in the lamplight. The escapement was the hard part. She tried three
different pallets before the tick settled into a steady rhythm, and when
it did, the sound filled the little shop like a second heartbeat.

By midnight the clock was running. It lost eleven seconds in the first
hour, then nine, then seven, as the oil worked into the pivots. She sat
beside it with a notebook of her own and wrote down the numbers, and
underneath them, in the margin, she drew a small sparrow.

In the morning the rain had stopped. Light came through the high windows
in long clean bars, and the dust moved slowly through it. The clock ticked
on, patient and unhurried, as if it had been waiting all these years for
someone to simply finish the work.
